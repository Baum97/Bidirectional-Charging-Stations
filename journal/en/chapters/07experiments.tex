\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

The experiment is conducted on mobility data, which is generated by an algorithm that simulates movement patterns of agents in a given area. The algorithm takes into account various factors such as land use, transportation infrastructure and natural behavior of agents to generate realistic mobility data. The generated data is then used to evaluate the performance of the proposed charging station placement algorithm. Before running the simulation, the dataset consists of surface level mobility (agent start- and endpoints, OSM-data), which is used for traffic flow, POIs and electrical grid information. When a simulation is run, the dataset is enriched with additional information such as charging behavior, energy consumption, specific locations of agents and their SoC. The datasets are generated using a combination of real-world data and synthetic data, ensuring that the results are both realistic and generalizable to other urban areas. The datasets are also designed to be scalable, allowing for the evaluation of the placement algorithms on larger areas with more agents and charging stations.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The reference scenario covers a one kilometer by one kilometer area in Berlin, Germany. Each agent follows a daily trip chain with departures spread across the day and returns to its home location between activities. The simulation covers 24 hours of simulated time with a time step of 10\,s, so the chains complete within the horizon and the evening period at home is part of the observation. The trip generator is selected by the pipeline from the configured duration: horizons below ten hours use a compressed departure window intended for short test runs, longer horizons use the daily generator applied here.

Every experiment is repeated over five random seeds, which redraw home and activity locations, the assignment of electric vehicles, and departure times. Across those five populations the scenario holds 236.6 $\pm$ 3.4 agents, of which 142.6 $\pm$ 1.8 are electric vehicles with a battery capacity of 80\,kWh initialised at 50\,\% \ac{SoC}; the remainder are conventional vehicles. Every agent carries a \ac{SUMO} battery device, because the simulation is configured with \texttt{device.battery.probability} $=1$, so the fleet-wide \ac{SoC} statistics cover all agents that entered the network, while charging is restricted to the electric vehicles. Half of the electric vehicle owners are assigned a private home wallbox at their home lane. The wallboxes are deliberately excluded from the additional file handed to \ac{SUMO} and exist only inside \ac{TraCI}, which restricts each of them to its owning vehicle type. As a result \ac{SUMO}'s stationfinder cannot route any vehicle to a private wallbox, and home charging occurs only when an agent is parked at its own home location.

Charging power, \ac{V2G} discharge and the wallbox logic are injected at runtime through \ac{TraCI}, which overrides the station power, applies the ramp-up, and arbitrates the total power draw against a synthetic distribution grid modelled in PandaPower with 37 buses, 93 lines and 10 transformers. Charging power per station is capped at 200\,kW with a 20\,s ramp-up, \ac{V2G} discharge is enabled above 50\,\% \ac{SoC} with up to 50\,kW per vehicle and is triggered when the grid reaches 90\,\% of its capacity. The grid capacity is derived from the area of the scenario at a density of 1500\,kW per square kilometer, giving 1500\,kW here.

The public infrastructure of the reference case is the one already present in the area, that is, the 26 charging stations extracted from \ac{OSM}. Each station is projected onto the nearest road segment whose lane is long enough to hold it, which keeps all 26 stations in the network: placing them on the closest segment regardless of its length puts several of them on junction stubs of a few decimetres, where the resulting extent is degenerate and rejected.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Repeated executions of an identical configuration were initially found to differ, with a spread of roughly 7\,\% in public and 9\,\% in private charging energy. The cause is the iteration order over the set of active electric vehicles, which determines the order in which vehicles request power from the shared pool and which varies between processes because Python randomises string hashing. Fixing the interpreter hash seed makes a run bit-identical, so all runs reported here are executed with a fixed hash seed and the variation between them is due to the scenario seed alone.

\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:results}

\subsubsection{The existing infrastructure}

\paragraph{Where the energy is drawn.} Over the simulated day the fleet draws 3853\,kWh, of which public stations deliver 3251.7 $\pm$ 315.4\,kWh in 246.2 $\pm$ 9.2 sessions to 98.2 $\pm$ 1.5 vehicles and private wallboxes deliver 601.5 $\pm$ 258.2\,kWh in 27.0 $\pm$ 6.5 sessions. Public charging thus accounts for 84\,\% of the energy. The imbalance follows from how the two kinds of infrastructure are reachable. Public stations are visible to the stationfinder and are approached whenever a vehicle runs low on charge, whereas a wallbox can only be used when its owner happens to be parked at home, which the spread of departure and return times makes comparatively rare within the horizon. The large relative spread of the wallbox figures, 43\,\% of their own mean, reflects exactly that: whether home charging happens at all depends on which agents a given seed places where. Every wallbox session is attributable to the owning agent, so the type restriction on private stations holds.

\paragraph{Accessibility.} Despite the public charging that does take place, the fleet ends the day well below its starting point. The mean \ac{SoC} drops from 50.0\,\% to 28.0 $\pm$ 0.7\,\%, with a median of 33.9 $\pm$ 2.9\,\%. Of all agents, 43.9 $\pm$ 1.2\,\% end below 20\,\% \ac{SoC}, 39.0 $\pm$ 2.2\,\% drop below 10\,\% at some point during the day, and 3.8 $\pm$ 0.3\,\% are fully depleted. \Cref{tab:baseline} summarises the reference case.

\paragraph{Bidirectional operation.} \ac{V2G} discharge is enabled but contributes no energy in the reference case, and remains negligible in every configuration studied here. Discharge requires a vehicle above 50\,\% \ac{SoC}, while the fleet ends the day at a mean of 28.0\,\% and the vehicles occupying the stations are there precisely because they are low. The framework therefore demonstrates that the mechanism is in place; quantifying what it is worth requires a fleet segment that is parked at a high state of charge when it is needed, and is left to future work.

\subsubsection{Derived charging demand}

The DBSCAN stage of the Clustering strategy operates on the samples in which a vehicle is below 30\,\% \ac{SoC}; half of them are drawn at random to bound the clustering cost. Clusters that fall within 100\,m of an already existing station are discarded, and across the five seeds the stage retains 29.4 $\pm$ 3.8 clusters. These are then turned into 34.6 $\pm$ 5.0 charging stations, one per cluster plus one for every further 50 estimated charge points, which is what raises the network from 26 to 60.6 $\pm$ 5.0 stations. A sample is one vehicle at one 10\,s time step, so the sample count measures dwell time rather than demand, and a vehicle parked at home for hours contributes thousands of samples without ever asking for a public charge. The demand of a cluster is therefore derived from the vehicles themselves. Samples inside a cluster are grouped per vehicle and split into separate visits whenever a vehicle is absent for more than five minutes, and each visit is charged with the energy it would need to reach 80\,\% \ac{SoC}. \Cref{tab:clusters} lists the ten strongest clusters of one representative seed. Demand is spread over many sites rather than concentrated in a few, and the mean \ac{SoC} inside a cluster is well below the 30\,\% threshold, which indicates that the retained clusters mark genuine range emergencies rather than opportunistic stops. Cluster radii stay compact, so each cluster is small enough to be served by a single station site.

\subsubsection{Comparison of the placement strategies}
\label{subsec:comparison}

Saturate-and-Prune places a candidate on every road segment long enough to hold one, which yields 196 candidates on top of the 26 existing stations, and then removes the least utilised half of the remaining candidates in each iteration. Running the loop for six iterations traces the network down from 222 to 33 stations and yields an accessibility curve against station count rather than a single operating point. Clustering derives its candidates from the demand log of the reference run in a single pass and ends at 60.6 $\pm$ 5.0 stations, which places it between the fourth and third iteration of that curve. \Cref{fig:placement_curve} shows both.

\paragraph{Adding stations closes most of the gap, and the method used to add them does not matter.} Any configuration with more than about fifty stations raises the mean end \ac{SoC} from 28.0\,\% to roughly 31\,\%, lifts the median by ten points, and reduces the share of agents ending below 20\,\% \ac{SoC} from 43.9\,\% to between 35 and 37\,\%. These effects are three to seven times the spread across seeds and are the clearest result of this study. At its own station count, however, Clustering lands squarely on the Saturate-and-Prune curve. Interpolating that curve to 60.6 stations gives a mean \ac{SoC} of roughly 30.9\,\% against the 31.4 $\pm$ 1.0\,\% Clustering achieves, a difference of half a standard deviation, and on the two tail metrics the ordering reverses: Clustering leaves 37.4 $\pm$ 1.7\,\% of agents below 20\,\% and 31.7 $\pm$ 2.4\,\% below 10\,\% where the interpolated curve gives roughly 36.6 and 29.9\,\%. Within the resolution of five seeds, the two strategies are equivalent, and what governs accessibility is how many stations are added rather than where a heuristic decides to put them.

\paragraph{Accessibility saturates well below full coverage.} From 222 stations down to 75 the mean end \ac{SoC} does not move at all, staying between 31.1 and 31.3\,\%, and the median even improves slightly as stations are removed. Degradation begins only below roughly fifty stations: at 39 stations the mean falls to 29.8 $\pm$ 0.9\,\% and the share of agents dropping below 10\,\% \ac{SoC} rises from 28.9 to 33.3\,\%. The useful operating range for this scenario is therefore between about fifty and seventy-five stations. Everything above it is redundant, which is why the fully saturated network of 222 stations performs no better than the 75 that survive three prune steps, and it is also why both strategies converge on comparable outcomes: they both land inside that plateau.

\paragraph{Clustering moves more energy for the same accessibility.} At comparable accessibility Clustering moves 5781 $\pm$ 530\,kWh through its public stations against roughly 4300\,kWh anywhere on the Saturate-and-Prune curve. Placing stations where vehicles are already at low \ac{SoC} produces longer and more energetic sessions than distributing them along the road network, but that additional energy does not translate into a better end-of-day \ac{SoC} for the fleet.

\paragraph{What separates the strategies is cost of computation, not quality of placement.} Clustering obtains its network from one simulation of the reference case. Saturate-and-Prune needs one simulation per prune iteration, six in the configuration reported here, and the station count it arrives at is determined by how many iterations are run rather than by its 5\,\% utilisation criterion, which almost never binds. For a planning workflow that difference is substantial. It is, on this evidence, the only defensible advantage of the demand-driven heuristic over naive saturation.

\paragraph{The utilisation threshold does not govern the outcome.} In the saturated network only one or two of 222 stations exceed 5\,\% occupancy, so the prune criterion selects almost everything for removal and the cap of one half per iteration is what actually determines the schedule. A sweep over the resulting networks shows why a different number would not repair this: 4.8 $\pm$ 2.6 stations lie above 5\,\% occupancy, 43.6 $\pm$ 1.5 above 1\,\%, and 48.6 $\pm$ 0.5 above 0.1\,\%. The distribution is bimodal, a station is either used or never touched, so any threshold between 1\,\% and 0.1\,\% yields nearly the same network. Saturate-and-Prune as implemented is thus better described as repeated halving guided by utilisation ranking than as a threshold rule.
