\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

The experiment is conducted on mobility data, which is generated by an algorithm that simulates movement patterns of agents in a given area. The algorithm takes into account various factors such as land use, transportation infrastructure and natural behavior of agents to generate realistic mobility data. The generated data is then used to evaluate the performance of the proposed charging station placement algorithm. Before running the simulation, the dataset consists of surface level mobility (agent start- and endpoints, OSM-data), which is used for traffic flow, POIs and electrical grid information. When a simulation is run, the dataset is enriched with additional information such as charging behavior, energy consumption, specific locations of agents and their SoC. The datasets are generated using a combination of real-world data and synthetic data, ensuring that the results are both realistic and generalizable to other urban areas. The datasets are also designed to be scalable, allowing for the evaluation of the placement algorithms on larger areas with more agents and charging stations.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The reference scenario covers a one kilometer by one kilometer area in Berlin, Germany. It is populated with 250 agents that are distributed across the area's residential zones and assigned activities and destinations from the generated trip chains. All agents are modelled as electric vehicles with a battery capacity of 80\,kWh, initialised at 50\,\% \ac{SoC}. The simulation covers one hour of simulated time with a time step of 10\,s, which keeps the coupled traffic and power-flow computation tractable while still resolving individual charging sessions. Charging power per station is capped at 200\,kW with a 20\,s ramp-up, \ac{V2G} discharge is enabled above 50\,\% \ac{SoC} with up to 50\,kW per vehicle, and 5\,\% of the agents own a private wallbox. The complete parameter set is persisted per run in \texttt{sim\_params.json}, so that every reported figure can be traced back to the configuration that produced it.

The results reported in \cref{subsec:results} are obtained from a single run with random seed 42 on the infrastructure that is already present in the area, that is, the 26 charging stations extracted from \ac{OSM}. This run serves two purposes: it quantifies the accessibility gap of the existing infrastructure, and it provides the charging-demand log from which the Clustering strategy derives its candidate sites. Because a single run cannot separate systematic effects from the noise that dynamic routing introduces, the numbers below are reported as observations of one scenario instance rather than as statistically significant differences between the two placement strategies. A multi-seed comparison of Saturate-and-Prune against Clustering along the metrics of \cref{subsec:eval_metrics} is the subject of ongoing work and is discussed in \cref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:results}

\paragraph{Accessibility of the existing infrastructure.} Over the simulated hour, the fleet's mean \ac{SoC} drops from 50.0\,\% to 33.9\,\%, with a large spread across agents (std.\ 19.6\,\%, median 43.4\,\%). The tail of that distribution is the relevant part: 28.0\,\% of all agents end the hour below 20\,\% \ac{SoC}, 22.8\,\% drop below 10\,\% at some point during the run, and 3.2\,\% are fully depleted. At the same time, only 6 of the 250 agents (2.4\,\%) manage to charge at all, and they do so at only 4 of the 26 available stations. The remaining 22 stations record no session, because they are not reachable along the routes the agents actually drive. The four used stations are occupied for 232 to 319 of the 360 simulation steps, that is, between 64\,\% and 89\,\% of the simulated hour, delivering 149.6\,kWh in total at an effective power of 47.5\,kW. This is the characteristic failure mode the placement algorithms are meant to address: the network is not short of stations in aggregate, it is short of stations where and when demand occurs, so a small subset saturates while the majority of the fleet is never served. \Cref{tab:baseline} summarises the run.

\paragraph{Derived charging demand.} The DBSCAN stage of the Clustering strategy condenses the 2249 logged low-\ac{SoC} samples into 21 spatially coherent demand clusters. A sample is one vehicle at one 10\,s time step below 30\,\% \ac{SoC}, so the sample count measures dwell time rather than demand: a single vehicle standing for 27 minutes contributes 163 samples. The demand of a cluster is therefore derived from the vehicles themselves. Samples inside a cluster are grouped per vehicle and split into separate visits whenever a vehicle is absent for more than five minutes, and each visit is charged with the energy it would need to reach 80\,\% \ac{SoC}. Across all clusters this yields 128 visits by 90 vehicles and an energy demand of 6393\,kWh. \Cref{tab:clusters} lists the ten clusters with the highest demand. The distribution is strongly skewed: the two largest clusters carry 55\,\% of all visits and 54\,\% of the energy demand, while 13 of the 21 clusters consist of a single visit by a single vehicle. The mean \ac{SoC} inside a cluster ranges from 5.5\,\% to 30.0\,\% (mean 18.7\,\%), which separates clusters that mark genuine range emergencies from those that merely mark opportunistic charging locations. Cluster radii stay compact at 25.0\,m to 66.6\,m (mean 31.1\,m), so each cluster is small enough to be served by a single station site rather than requiring a distributed set of sites. Two of the 21 clusters lie 3.3\,m apart and describe the same demand twice; discounting the duplicate, the non-redundant demand is 4680\,kWh.

\paragraph{Grid-aware siting.} All 21 clusters fall within reach of the synthetic distribution grid, at a mean distance of 105\,m to the nearest connection point (max.\ 249\,m). Nine clusters are rated \emph{excellent} and twelve \emph{good} with respect to available grid capacity; none had to be discarded for lack of a feasible connection. Four clusters are limited to a 500\,kW connection while the remaining seventeen have 5000\,kW available. A charge point is a single connector at which one vehicle can charge, so a station site aggregates several of them; the heuristic sizes a cluster as its hourly energy demand divided by the throughput of one 50\,kW charge point at 75\,\% utilisation, and then caps the result at what the local grid connection can supply. For the 21 retained clusters the grid cap is not binding, since the largest cluster requires 46 charge points against the 100 its 5000\,kW connection would allow; it did bind for three further clusters that were subsequently discarded for being too close to an existing station. The demand estimate over all clusters is 184 charge points, distributed over the 29 station sites the strategy generates, i.e.\ about six connectors per site. Neither the number of sites nor the grid connection is the limiting factor in this scenario: the binding constraint is that demand concentrates on very few locations, which \cref{tab:clusters} shows directly.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} Taken together, the run supports the premise of the approach but does not yet discriminate between the two placement strategies. The observed concentration of demand, with over half of all visits in two clusters and 85\,\% of the existing stations unused, is precisely the situation in which a traffic- and \ac{SoC}-driven heuristic is expected to outperform uniform saturation. Confirming that expectation requires the multi-seed comparison described in \cref{sec:discussion}; the numbers reported here establish the baseline against which it will be measured.
